The Big Three,
garage edition

Dumbbells, a Swiss ball and a medicine ball. Built to keep power and durability on the bike as the years add up.

A
Full body · squat focus
Tap any move for how-to & the one key cue
1Goblet squat3 × 8–12
Too easy? Swap to Bulgarian split squats, 3 × 8 per leg.

Hold one dumbbell vertically against your chest, cupped under the top end like a goblet, elbows tucked down. Feet a little wider than hips. Sit down and back as if lowering onto a low chair, chest up, knees tracking over your toes, until thighs are roughly parallel to the floor. Stand back up.

CueThe dumbbell at your chest keeps you upright and protects your lower back.
2Swiss ball dumbbell press3 × 8–10

Sit on the ball, walk your feet forward and roll down until just your upper back and head rest on it, hips lifted so your torso makes a flat table — this stands in for a bench. Dumbbells above your chest; press up until arms are straight, lower under control until elbows are level with your torso.

CueSqueeze your glutes the whole time to hold the bridge steady.
3Single-arm dumbbell row3 × 8–10 / arm

Place one knee and the same-side hand on the Swiss ball (or a chair) so your back is flat and roughly parallel to the floor. The dumbbell hangs from your free hand. Pull it up towards your hip, leading with the elbow and squeezing the shoulder blade, then lower fully.

CueKeep your back flat — don't twist your torso to heave the weight up.
4Swiss ball hamstring curl2–3 × 10–12

Lie on your back, heels and lower calves on top of the ball, arms flat for balance. Lift your hips into a bridge, then bend your knees to roll the ball in towards your backside, then roll it back out under control.

CueKeep your hips lifted throughout — that's what makes the hamstrings work.
5Medicine ball slam3 × 6–8
Power move. Don't skip it — explosive work fades faster with age than raw strength, and it carries straight over to sprints.

Stand tall, ball overhead in both hands. Reach up, then throw it down hard into the floor in front of you, hinging at the hips as you slam. Catch on the bounce or pick it up, and repeat.

CueSpeed and intent, not grinding — short, sharp, explosive reps.
B
Full body · hinge focus
Tap any move for how-to & the one key cue
1Dumbbell Romanian deadlift3 × 8–10
Want more balance work? Single-leg RDL, 3 × 8 per leg.

Stand holding the dumbbells in front of your thighs. With a soft, constant bend in the knees, push your hips backwards and let the dumbbells slide down the front of your legs, kept close, until you feel a stretch in your hamstrings (around shin height). Stand up by driving your hips forward.

CueIt's a hip hinge, not a squat — back flat, chest proud, motion from the hips.
2Standing overhead press3 × 6–8

Stand tall, dumbbells at shoulder height, palms facing forward. Press straight overhead until your arms lock out, then lower under control back to your shoulders.

CueBrace your stomach and squeeze your glutes so you don't arch your lower back to help.
3Reverse lunge3 × 8 / leg
Alternative: step-ups onto a solid box or low bench — drive through the top heel, lower under control.

From standing, step one foot back a stride's length and lower until both knees are bent around 90°, back knee hovering near the floor. Push through the front heel to step back up. Dumbbells at your sides.

CueStepping backwards, not forwards, is gentler on the knees.
4Swiss ball stir-the-pot2–3 sets
Simpler option: dead bug — on your back, lower opposite arm and leg while pressing your lower back flat to the floor.

Set your forearms on the ball in a plank, body in a straight line, feet on the floor. Keeping your torso rock-solid, use your forearms to move the ball in small circles, as if stirring a big pot.

CueSmall circles — the point is resisting the wobble, not making big movements.
5Med ball rotational throw3 × 6 / side
Power move. Explosive rotation from the hips — carries over to standing efforts and sprints.

Stand side-on to a solid wall, a step away, ball at waist height in both hands. Rotate away to wind up, then powerfully rotate your hips and torso towards the wall and throw the ball into it, catching the rebound. All reps one side, then turn around.

CueDrive the throw from the hips, not the arms.

Making it harder

With fixed dumbbells you can't just add plates. Work through these four levers in order before you buy heavier weights.

1Add reps, then a set

Climb to the top of the rep range across all sets, then add a whole set. This is double progression — reps first, then volume.

2Slow the lowering

Take a 3-second descent on every rep. A light dumbbell starts to feel a lot heavier when you can't rush the way down.

3Add a pause

Hold for a beat at the hardest point — the bottom of a split squat, the stretch of an RDL. No momentum to help you out of it.

4Go single-leg

Shift bilateral moves to one leg at a time. That roughly doubles the effective load per leg without needing a single extra kilo.

The 4-week rhythm

Run it in blocks. Weeks 1–3 add a little each week — a rep, a set, or a touch more load. Week 4 back right off to RPE 6 as a deload. Start the next block slightly harder than you began the last.

Warm-up & effort

The habits around the lifting matter as much as the lifting — especially for protecting your back.

Always warm up the main move

Anatoly's competitive career was effectively ended by lifting heavy cold for the cameras. Do the opposite: ramp up with a few progressively heavier sets before your first working set.

Empty / light~40%~60%~80%Working

Read effort with RPE

Forget chasing a one-rep max. Work at RPE 7–8 — finish each set with 2–3 reps still in the tank. Safer and more sustainable for a Masters athlete, and it still drives real gains.

Fit it around the bike

Keep heavy leg days away from your key interval or long rides — ideally on an easy or rest day. Your legs will feel heavy for the first couple of weeks; that settles, and the payoff is better sprint power and late-ride durability.

Feed the work

Older athletes face some anabolic resistance, so both the strength stimulus and protein matter more than they used to. A common sports-nutrition range is roughly 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight daily, spread across the day. General guidance, not a personalised prescription — treat it as a starting point.

A note on the plan. This is general fitness information rather than individual medical or physiotherapy advice. If a movement causes pain (as opposed to normal effort), stop and get it checked — especially anything involving your back.
